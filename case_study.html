Case study · FX forecasting

Which currency questions are actually answerable?

Ten years of EURUSD, USDJPY and USDINR, three different prediction targets, every model graded against a named baseline before it was built. Two of the three questions turned out to be dead ends. The third improves a rate that gets set every month.

What goes in

Public data only, lagged one day, matched to the horizon

Nothing here is proprietary or paid. Every input is free, reproducible, and available to anyone — which is deliberate: it sets an honest floor for what a rate signal can be worth before you buy data.

Two coverage gaps are stated rather than papered over: euro-area and US CPI are published on FRED while Japan and India CPI are not, so the inflation feature is limited to EURUSD; and no rupee futures contract exists, so USDINR sits out the positioning tier.

Guard 1

One-day lag on everything

Every feature is lagged one trading day, so a model only ever sees values published before the price it is predicting from. Enforced in the pipeline, not by convention.

Guard 2

Frequency matched to the question

Daily bars to predict the next day, weekly bars for the next week, monthly for the next month. Mixing frequencies is where look-ahead usually creeps in.

Guard 3

Retrained at every date

Walk-forward throughout: each model is refit as of each prediction date and never scored on data it was trained on.

Outcome 01 · the usable one

Fix next month's rate from today's close, not last month's average

Plenty of decisions need a single rate agreed in advance — a pricing rate, a planning rate, a rate written into a contract. What those decisions need is next month's average, which is a different target from next month's closing price, with a different correct baseline. It is rarely tested as its own question.

Three ways of setting that number were compared at each month-end across 104 months (2017–2026): carry forward last month's average (the common habit), carry forward today's rate, and a small statistical model. Error is measured as a percentage of the average that actually occurred.

The operational change

Reset the forward rate at month-end from the latest close instead of the trailing average.

No model to deploy, no data to buy, no latency budget — and it held on all three pairs tested.

Outcome 02 · the forecastable one

Size is predictable where direction is not

Ask how big next week's moves will be rather than which way they go, and the problem becomes tractable. A standard volatility model (HAR — yesterday's, last week's and last month's volatility as inputs) roughly halved the error of assuming today's turbulence continues, on all three pairs.

What you would do with it

Knowing next week is likely to be turbulent does not tell you which way the rate will move. It tells you how much cushion to leave.

In practice: a wider spread, an earlier hedge, a smaller position, or a tighter limit for that week — and the reverse in a week forecast to be calm.

Outcome 03 · the dead ends

Nothing beat "the next period repeats the last one"

Fifteen models were graded on direction — trend rules, carry, institutional and retail playbooks, logistic regression, gradient boosting, random forest, a hidden Markov model, an ensemble. Across the held-out final period, none produced an edge whose confidence interval excluded zero.

One result did survive, and it is about confidence rather than accuracy: the most complex model's probabilities were measurably worse-calibrated than the naive floor's on every pair — far more confident than its hit rate justified. Scoring probability quality, not just accuracy, is what surfaced that.

A second dead end was tested and documented rather than dropped: 99 post-meeting FOMC statements were scored hawkish, neutral or dovish and fed to the models across 402 identical weeks. The scorer works as a measurement — it reads the 2022–23 hiking cycle as hawkish and the 2020 emergency cuts as dovish. As a predictor it added nothing in either direction, so the features were rejected. Full numbers in the appendix.

What this rules out

Rate-prediction features

Ten years of public data give no reliable basis for a "the rate will move this way next" signal at day, week or month horizons. Worth knowing before it reaches a roadmap.

What this supports

Volatility-aware pricing

Forecast the size of the move and let it set spread, timing and limits. That is the part of the problem public data actually answers.

What it costs to check

Two weeks, free data

Every result here comes from public sources and open-source tooling. Ruling something out cheaply is itself a deliverable.

How it was tested

Baselines named before models were built

A named floor for every target

Persistence and majority-class for direction, carry-forward volatility for turbulence, carry-forward rate for averages. Half the findings here are baseline corrections.

Intervals on every headline claim

Block-bootstrap confidence intervals throughout, with sample sizes stated. Small samples are labelled as anecdotes rather than quoted as results.

Confidence scored, not assumed

Probability quality is graded alongside accuracy, and overconfident models are recalibrated on held-out data or shown raw and labelled where the sample is too small.

Fully reproducible

Data downloads, feature pipeline, model bench and every chart regenerate from scripts in one repository. Built with pandas, scikit-learn and hmmlearn.

A.2 — Central-bank language as a model input

Statements were scored with a transparent, deterministic dictionary method rather than a black-box model, so any reader can check why a statement scored the way it did.

Both intervals span zero. The scorer partially validates as a measurement — it tracks the 2022–23 hiking cycle and the 2020 emergency cuts correctly, and misses pivots that lived in decisions rather than adjectives. It does not validate as a predictor, and the features were rejected on that basis rather than retained because they were interesting to build.

A.3 — Known limits of this study

  • Three pairs only — two major, one emerging-market. Findings should not be assumed to carry to thin or managed crosses
  • Volatility is proxied from daily closes rather than measured intraday
  • Inflation features exist for EURUSD only, since Japan and India CPI are not on the same public source
  • No rupee futures contract exists, so USDINR has no fund-positioning input
  • Central-bank language covers the dollar leg only — RBI statements were not machine-collectable
  • No transaction costs, spreads or slippage are modelled; a statistical edge is not a net-of-cost edge

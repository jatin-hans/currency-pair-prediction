Case study · Currency forecasting

Which currency questions are actually answerable?

Ten years of daily prices for the euro, the yen and the rupee against the dollar. Three different questions asked of the same data, every answer graded against a stated simple guess. Two of the three questions turned out to be dead ends. The third improves a rate that gets set every month.

What goes in

Free public data - and the bug that nearly fooled us

Nothing here is proprietary or paid. Daily prices come from Yahoo Finance (2015 onward), interest rates and inflation from FRED (the US Federal Reserve's public database), weekly positions of large trading funds from a US regulator, and the Fed's own policy statements. That is deliberate: it shows what currency forecasting can deliver before anyone spends money on data.

The timestamp bug

Early on, one model looked 70% accurate at predicting the next day - implausibly good. The cause turned out to be a timestamp mismatch: Yahoo stamps its currency prices earlier in the day than it stamps the dollar index. Join the two on the same date, and “today's” dollar move secretly contains information about tomorrow's euro move. The model wasn't smart; it was peeking.

The check that caught it uses correlation - a score from −1 to +1 where near 0 means “no connection” and near −1 or +1 means “strongly connected”:

eur = log(closes["EURUSD"]).diff() # the euro's daily moves dxy = log(closes["DXY"]).diff() # the dollar index's daily moves corr(dxy, eur) # -0.09 -> no connection, as expected corr(dxy, eur.shift(-1)) # -0.87 -> strongly connected to TOMORROW's move = a leak # fix: lag the index series one day, so a model only ever sees # values that existed before the price it predicts from dxy = closes["DXY"].shift(1)

From notebooks/01_direction.ipynb, section 1

After the one-day shift, the 70% “accuracy” collapsed to ordinary guessing. Every number on this page comes from the fixed data - and finding this bug is, honestly, the most transferable result of the whole project: when a forecast looks too good for its field, audit the timestamps first.

Guard 1

One-day lag on everything

Every input is shifted one trading day, so a model only ever sees values published before the price it is predicting from. Enforced in code, not by convention.

Guard 2

Frequency matched to the question

Daily data predicts the next day, weekly data the next week, monthly the next month. Mixing frequencies is where accidental peeking usually creeps in.

Guard 3

Tested only on unseen data

Models slide forward through time, re-trained at each date, and are always scored on data they have never seen - the way real forecasting works.

The shape of the problem

One chart that predicts the whole study

Before building any model, ask the price history a simple question: “does what happened in the last few days tell you anything about today?” Ask it twice - once about the direction of moves, once about their size.

The blue bars sit at zero on every pair: yesterday's direction tells you nothing about today's. (One blue bar on the rupee panel dips below zero - the rupee tends to give back a little the day after a move - but it is too small and short-lived to build on, and the models later confirmed that.) The orange bars are clearly positive everywhere: wild days follow wild days, calm days follow calm days.

In plain terms: the market hides which way it will move, but telegraphs how rough the ride will be. That one difference decides all three verdicts below.

Outcome 01 · the usable one

Plan next month around today's rate, not last month's average

Businesses constantly need one exchange rate agreed in advance - a budgeting rate, a pricing rate, a rate written into a contract. What that number is really trying to guess is next month's average rate, and it is rarely tested as its own question.

We compared three ways of setting it, at every month-end across 104 months (2017-2026). The score is simple: on average, how far off was the guess, as a percentage of the average that actually happened? Smaller is better.

The whole test fits in a few lines - at each month-end, record what each method would have guessed, then compare with the average that actually happened:

for each month_end: guess_a = average(prices, last_month) # the common habit guess_b = price_today # just today's rate guess_c = ar_model(recent_moves).forecast() # continue recent behaviour forward actual = average(prices, next_month) # known a month later record(percent_miss(guess, actual) for each guess)

Condensed from scripts/14_period_average.py - full version in the repo

Guess C - the statistical model - ties guess B everywhere. That is the punchline: the win comes from picking a better starting point, not from modelling.

The operational change

At each month-end, set the planning rate from today's rate instead of last month's average.

No model to deploy, no data to buy - and it held on all three pairs tested.

Outcome 02 · the forecastable one

Size is predictable where direction is not

“Volatility” just means how much the price jumps around - a calm week versus a wild one. Knowing next week will be wild doesn't say which way prices go, but it tells a business how much safety margin to leave: buy protection sooner, charge a bigger buffer, put less money at stake that week.

Two forecasters were compared one week ahead, on 426 separate weeks per pair. The do-nothing guess: assume next week is as wild as the recent past. The volatility model: a standard statistics-textbook recipe (called HAR) that blends yesterday's, last week's and last month's choppiness into one forecast - a weighted average whose weights are learned from history:

# three views of recent choppiness ... vol_yesterday = abs(move_yesterday) vol_last_week = typical_move(last_5_days) vol_last_month = typical_move(last_21_days) # ... blended into one forecast, weights learned from history next_week_vol = w1*vol_yesterday + w2*vol_last_week + w3*vol_last_month

The HAR recipe, condensed from scripts/10_turbulence_har.py

Result: the model cuts the forecast error roughly in half on every pair - 51-57% less error, measured by the scoring rule statisticians use for volatility forecasts (it punishes under-warning about a storm more than over-warning about one).

A horizon is how far ahead the forecast looks - here, one week. The error score is the volatility scoring rule described above; its units only matter for comparison - lower is better. Complexity has to earn its place, and here it did not. Monthly results and a measurement note are in the appendix.

What you would do with it

Knowing next week is likely to be rough doesn't tell you which way the rate moves. It tells you how much cushion to leave.

In practice: buy protection sooner, price in a bigger buffer, or put less at stake in a week forecast to be wild - and the reverse in a week forecast calm.

Outcome 03 · the dead ends

Nothing beat “the next period repeats the last one”

Fifteen approaches were graded on direction - classic trading rules, statistical models, machine-learning models, and combinations. Every one raced the laziest possible guess: assume the next period does what the last one did.

Accuracy is scored out of 100, so “+6.4” below means the best model was right about 6 more times per 100 guesses than lazy guessing. In every case that advantage was smaller than the uncertainty around it - it could easily be luck:

The one thing the failure taught

Some models state a confidence with every guess (“70% sure it goes up”). We checked whether those confidences are honest: when a model says 70%, is it right about 70% of the time?

The simple model (logistic regression) sits near the line - its confidence means something. The complex one (gradient boosting) is far off it: when it said “90% sure”, it was right barely half the time. A model can look fine on accuracy and still be dangerously overconfident - you only see it if you check. Anyone who acts on a stated probability should care about this chart more than any accuracy table.

Do the Fed's words help?

The second dead end was tested rather than assumed. The Fed's rate-setting committee (the FOMC) publishes a statement after each meeting; we scored all 99 of them from hawkish (leaning toward higher interest rates) to dovish (leaning toward cuts) with a transparent dictionary method - counting phrases, so any reader can check why a statement scored the way it did:

hawkish_phrases = ["inflation remains elevated", "tightening", "restrictive", ...] dovish_phrases = ["accommodative", "downside risks", "cut the target", ...] score = (hawkish_hits - dovish_hits) / (hawkish_hits + dovish_hits) # score runs -1 (fully dovish) .. +1 (fully hawkish)

Condensed from scripts/12_stance_score.py

So the measurement works. But fed into the models across the same 402 weeks, the scores changed nothing - predictions got no better with them than without. The input was rejected, and the experiment kept in the repo as a documented negative. A measurement can be valid and still add no forecasting value; both facts are worth publishing.

What this rules out

Rate-direction inputs

Ten years of public data give no reliable basis for a “the rate will move this way next” prediction at day, week or month horizons. Worth knowing before it reaches a roadmap.

What this supports

Roughness-aware planning

Forecast the size of the moves and let it set buffers, timing and limits. That is the part of the problem public data actually answers.

What it costs to check

Free data, open tools

Every result here comes from public sources and open-source tooling, and reruns from the repository. Ruling something out cheaply is itself a deliverable.

How it was tested

Baselines named before models were built

A named “lazy guess” for every question

Repeat-the-last-period for direction, assume-it-continues for volatility, carry-forward for the planning rate. Beat the lazy guess or it doesn't count - half of what this study found came from fixing the comparison, not the model.

Uncertainty stated on every claim

Every headline number carries a statistical uncertainty range and its sample size. Small samples are called anecdotes, not results.

Confidence scored, not assumed

Stated probabilities are graded against reality. Overconfident models get their confidence corrected using data they haven't seen - or are shown as-is, with a warning attached.

Fully reproducible

Data, pipeline, models and every chart on this page regenerate from the repository - two notebooks tell the story end to end, numbered scripts rebuild every table.

A.2 - The Fed-language numbers

The direction models were run with and without the Fed-language inputs on the same 402 weeks. One note so the numbers below don't surprise you: these are three-way guesses - up, down, or roughly flat - so random guessing scores about 33%, and numbers below 50% are normal, not broken. Accuracy barely moved, in opposite directions for the two models, and the statistical uncertainty around both differences includes zero - the definition of “no measurable effect”.

A.3 - Known limits of this study

  • Three pairs only - two heavily traded, one from a developing economy. Findings should not be assumed to carry to other currencies
  • Roughness is estimated from end-of-day prices rather than measured minute by minute
  • No rupee version of the fund-positioning data exists, so USDINR sits that input out
  • Central-bank language covers the Fed only - the Indian central bank's statements were not machine-collectable
  • No transaction costs are modelled; this is about forecast quality, not a trading strategy
